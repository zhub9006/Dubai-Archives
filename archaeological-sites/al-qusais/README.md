> **Dubai, United Arab Emirates | c. 2500–550 BCE**
> *One of the most important prehistoric sites in the Arabian Peninsula*

## ⏳ Chronological Overview

The site spans two major prehistoric periods:

| Period | Date Range (BCE) | Cultural Horizon |
|---|---|---|
| Late Bronze Age | c. 2000–1300 | Wadi Suq period |
| Iron Age I | c. 1300–1000 | Early Iron Age |
| Iron Age II | c. 1000–550 | Late Iron Age |

The site is broadly dated **c. 2500–550 BCE**, making it a continuous record of human occupation spanning nearly 2,000 years.

---

## 🗺️ Site Areas

### 1. Bronze and Iron Age Necropolis
The primary burial ground, comprising:
- **267 simple pit graves** and **3 communal/collective tombs** (290+ total documented)
- Graves contain **flexed skeletons in lateral positions**
- Rich grave goods recovered across all excavation phases
- Evidence of **familial burial customs**: children (aged ~2–6 years) found interred alongside adults, positioned near the adult's abdomen

### 2. Mound of Serpents *(Tell al-Hayat)*
An **Iron Age II occupation area (c. 1000–800 BCE)** distinguished by:
- Numerous **copper alloy snake figurines**
- **Ceramics adorned with snake motifs**
- A **columned hall structure** interpreted as a possible early temple or ritual space
- Parallels to Iron Age snake-cult evidence at **Bithnah (Fujairah)**, **Masafi**, and sites in **Oman and Bahrain**

> 💡 *The Mound of Serpents provides some of the clearest evidence for serpent veneration or ritual cult practice in south-eastern Arabia during the Iron Age.*

## ⛏️ Excavation History

### 1970s — First Discovery
- **Discovery year:** 1974
- **Lead archaeologist:** Munir Taha (Iraqi archaeologist)
- **Organisation:** Dubai Municipality
- **Key findings:**
  - First systematic exposure of the necropolis
  - Identification of 267 pit graves and 3 communal tombs
  - Discovery of the Mound of Serpents
  - Recovery of copper snake figurines and snake-decorated ceramics
  - Evidence of columned hall (possible Iron Age II temple)

### 1990s — Renewed Excavation
- **Years:** 1991–1993
- **Lead archaeologist:** H. Qandil
- **Organisation:** Dubai Department of Antiquities and Heritage
- **Key findings:**
  - 101 additional burial pits excavated
  - Rich grave good assemblages recovered but **not fully published at the time**
  - Human skeletal remains identified; study delayed ~30 years

### 2020 — Reassessment Campaign
- **Lead archaeologist:** Tatiana Valente (Sanisera International Archaeology Institute)
- **Organisation:** Dubai Culture Authority & Arts / Sanisera International Archaeology Institute
- **Key findings:**
  - 9 new burial pits excavated; 19 new pits identified in total
  - First systematic analysis of the 1990s unanalysed material
  - Grave goods confirmed: **copper daggers, soft-stone (chlorite/steatite) vessels, pottery, jewellery, beads, shells**
  - Human remains formally identified and analysed
  - **Geomorphological and palaeoenvironmental study** of the prehistoric landscape
  - Refined chronological framework for the Bronze-to-Iron Age cultural transition
  - **Infant and child burial study**: 7 child remains in 126 excavated graves

> 📄 *Primary publication: Valente et al. (2023), Proceedings of the Seminar for Arabian Studies, Vol. 52, pp. 355–372*

### 2024–Ongoing — Active Research Programme
- **Organisation:** Dubai Culture Authority & Arts / Sanisera International Archaeology Institute (Limited Tender No. 38 of 2024)
- **Key findings:**
  - Updated burial chronology published
  - GIS-based palaeoenvironmental reconstructions
  - Site formally recognised as one of the **most important prehistoric sites in the Arabian Peninsula**

## 🌟 Cultural Significance

### Regional Importance
Al Qusais is recognised as **one of the most important prehistoric archaeological sites in the Arabian Peninsula**, offering a rare, continuous stratigraphic record from the Late Bronze Age through to the Iron Age.

### Burial Practices & Community Life
The necropolis provides detailed insights into:
- **Funerary customs** of Bronze-to-Iron Age Gulf communities
- **Demographic patterns**, including evidence of high infant/child mortality
- **Familial burial arrangements** (children interred beside adults)
- **Social organisation** and community structure

### Trade Networks
Grave goods — particularly **imported soft-stone vessels** and **copper weapons** — indicate robust trade connections across the Arabian Peninsula, consistent with the Wadi Suq cultural horizon and broader Gulf exchange networks.

### Bronze-to-Iron Age Cultural Transition
Al Qusais is a key reference site for understanding the **poorly-differentiated cultural shift** from the Bronze Age to the Iron Age in the UAE, a transition still under active scholarly investigation.

### The Serpent Cult
The Mound of Serpents provides rare archaeological evidence for **serpent-related ritual practices** in Iron Age II south-eastern Arabia. Parallels at Bithnah (Fujairah) and Oman suggest a regional phenomenon linked to water, copper, and religious symbolism.
